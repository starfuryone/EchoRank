## What the experiments found

Two controlled tests tracked 775 citation events across ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Perplexity. The first experiment, conducted over several months, achieved roughly 10 to 12 appearances for 15 commercial-intent keywords. The second, a 30-day cold-start test on a SaaS agency with no measurable AI presence at the outset, used the lessons from the first run. One finding from the first test did not survive the second, which is why structured experiments matter more than isolated screenshots or theory.

## Listicles on already-cited sources drove three-quarters of all citations

[According to Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/geo-experiments-challenge-conventional-ai-visibility-advice-488342), listicles accounted for 72.4% of citations, PR for 24.1%, and guest posts, owned site, and LinkedIn split the remainder. The leading source was a comprehensive listicle on Indeed SEO, which generated 190 mentions—more than all other sources combined. The key detail: Indeed SEO was already being cited by ChatGPT around 10 times before the placement went live.

This is not a coincidence. Our recommendation is to research which sources AI models already cite for your keyword targets, then pitch those publications first. A placement on a source with existing AI visibility compounds faster than outreach to new domains. When you secure that placement, it remains visible for months. When you try to build visibility through sources the models do not yet know, you are fighting the current.

The experiment also found that listicles and PR functioned as one system rather than separate channels. The listicle introduced the claim, the press release reinforced it, guest posts referenced both. Each layer performed better when supported by the others. This suggests that one-off placements, no matter how well-written, are unlikely to sustain visibility alone. We recommend treating GEO placements as part of a coordinated campaign, not as individual assets.

## Source authority and relevance mattered more than content depth

The experiments revealed a practical hierarchy: industry placements supported by PR produced strong results in the first test, while better-written listicles on general sites produced little visibility in the second. This challenges the assumption that superior content quality compensates for weak source authority.

Depth appeared to matter, but only on sources that already had authority. The first five listicles in the experiment covered the topic only briefly, and most generated little visibility. The one comprehensive piece continued to earn citations. However, a hypothesis emerges: it may not be content depth alone that drove those citations, but the combination of depth on a source the models already trusted.

The peer set also influenced visibility. One listicle placed the brand alongside recognized industry names like Lily Ray and Aleyda Solis. After those names were removed, performance declined within days. This suggests that models may evaluate the surrounding entities, not only the individual mention. A similar pattern appeared in PR, where being named alongside recognized experts outperformed a standalone feature on a stronger outlet.

## Most placements lost visibility within 30 days

Roughly half of the sources stopped being cited within 30 days. One MEXC placement fell from 29 mentions to 11 week over week; a Triple Review listicle that had been cited consistently declined without any direct intervention. This raises an important question: we cannot yet verify whether those declines were due to model retraining, citation refreshes, or deprioritization. The pattern suggests that one-time publication is unlikely to sustain visibility on its own, and that continuous amplification or updates may be necessary to maintain presence.

Our recommendation is to plan for visibility decay and treat initial placements as the start of a campaign, not the finish line. Monitor your citations across platforms using [our free audit tool](/free-audit) to catch these declines early.

## Query type, capitalization, and exact-match keywords still mattered

The experiments found that query type influenced source selection. Software and tool queries favored high-authority review sites, while service queries more often returned listicles. Matching the placement type to the query appeared to improve the likelihood of being cited.

The brand ranked for "Best LLM SEO Consultant" but barely appeared for "Best AI SEO Consultant," despite similar intent. The first phrase had a dedicated listicle, while the second did not. In this test, broader semantic coverage did not bridge the gap, suggesting that high-value commercial keywords may require dedicated assets. This is a hypothesis worth testing on your own keywords, particularly if you are targeting multiple phrasings of the same intent.

Capitalization also affected which sources were retrieved. Capitalized and lowercase versions returned different citations in three repeated tests, although this finding requires further validation. This is a small detail that most GEO advice overlooks, and we recommend testing it on your own highest-value keywords.

## SERP visibility still influenced LLM citations

SERP visibility appeared to influence AI visibility, especially in ChatGPT. When the model relied on web search to resolve a query, brands absent from the retrieved results were also absent from the answer. This finding connects traditional SEO to GEO in a way that many practitioners have theorized but fewer have measured. Our recommendation is to maintain SEO discipline alongside your GEO work, particularly for keywords where you see the model citing web results directly.

Pages performed better when the answer appeared within the first 100 words. A key takeaway block near the top produced a larger improvement than any other on-page change. ## Why the second test mattered

The original plan for the second experiment was to test specific tactics: Person schema, LinkedIn cadence, YouTube push. Instead, the researcher ran the entire playbook from zero on a different brand in a different vertical. This is a far stronger test of whether findings are generalizable or artifact of the first campaign.

One of the original conclusions from the first test did not hold in the second. The report does not name which finding failed, but it emphasizes that theory and isolated tests are not enough. Structured, repeated experiments expose what actually works. Most of the current GEO advice circulating online is based on isolated screenshots, single campaigns, or extrapolation from theory. Measurable, tracked results are rare. That is why these two experiments, though limited to one researcher and one set of keywords, offer more practical value than much of what passes for GEO guidance.

Our recommendation is to run similar experiments on your own keywords and verticals. Generative engine optimization is still young enough that findings from one domain may not transfer to another. Track your citations manually or with consistent tools. Document what changed between tests. Share those results.

## How to apply this to your work

- Identify which sources AI models already cite for your target keywords
- Pitch those sources first, before approaching new domains
- Plan placements as part of an integrated campaign, not as one-off assets
- Pair listicles with PR and guest posts that reference them
- Match placement type to query type (reviews for software, listicles for services)
- Monitor your citations across platforms weekly, not monthly
- Plan for visibility decay and treat initial placements as the start, not the end
- Test capitalization and exact-match keywords on high-value queries
- Maintain SEO discipline for keywords where models cite web results directly
- Put your strongest hook in the first 100 words of the page
