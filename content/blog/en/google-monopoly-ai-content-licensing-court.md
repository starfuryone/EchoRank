## Google's search monopoly may not protect its AI content use

[According to Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/judge-in-penske-vs-google-says-ai-situation-seems-really-unfair/587216/), Judge Amit Mehta questioned whether Google's dominance in search gives it an unfair advantage in training AI systems. At a Tuesday hearing on Penske Media's antitrust lawsuit, Mehta pushed back on Google's argument that AI Overviews is merely a "product improvement" immune from antitrust law. The judge noted that competitors like OpenAI and Perplexity pay millions for content licensing, while Google uses its search monopoly to ingest publisher material at zero cost. He described the situation as "really unfair" and said product improvements are not exempt from antitrust scrutiny.

This hearing marks a potential inflection point for how courts view the relationship between search dominance and AI training practices.

## What Mehta's skepticism signals about Google's legal exposure

Judge Mehta's comments suggest he may not accept Google's core defense. Google has argued that publishers can opt out of indexing or disable AI Mode, and that the traditional arrangement—crawl access in exchange for search traffic—remains valid. Mehta rejected this framing. He noted that publishers lack meaningful control over how Google uses their content and that AI Overviews are built "on the backs of the publishers." His observation about other AI companies paying for access while Google pays nothing points to a gap in competitive fairness that existing antitrust law may address. A hypothesis worth watching: if Mehta lets Penske's case proceed past the motion-to-dismiss stage, it could open a broader discovery process that exposes how Google trains its AI systems on publisher data.

Google truthfully notes that publishers can now opt out of AI Mode and AI Overviews. But the real-world choice is harsh. Publishers that opt out lose whatever minimal traffic AI Overviews sends—which is far less than what Google's traditional "ten blue links" once delivered.

## How this ruling could reshape publisher visibility strategies

Our recommendation: treat AI visibility as a separate problem from traditional search visibility. The two are no longer the same thing. When Google shows an AI Overview instead of a traditional search result, publishers see traffic drop sharply. The court's suggestion that Google cannot unilaterally use publisher content for AI training without some form of compensation or control may eventually force Google to offer publishers a choice between traditional search placement and AI training—but this remains a hypothesis at this stage. Mehta has not yet ruled on Google's motion to dismiss.

In the interim, publishers should document how much traffic they lose to AI Overviews by reviewing query patterns and click-through rates. ## Why the content licensing asymmetry matters for your authority

The judge highlighted a structural problem: Google's search monopoly creates a moat around its AI training costs. Perplexity, OpenAI, and other AI companies license content from publishers and distributors because they have no search dominance to leverage. Google, by contrast, can use search indexing requirements as a backdoor content acquisition mechanism. Publishers index their content to be found by Google's search engine; Google then uses that same indexed content to train and power its AI systems without a separate licensing agreement. This arrangement favors Google and raises entry barriers for competitors, which is precisely what antitrust law is designed to prevent.

Your site's content is valuable to multiple parties. Search engines have one interest. AI training platforms have another. The fact that Google treats them as a single transaction is what troubles Mehta.

## How AI licensing could reshape publisher economics

If the court finds that Google cannot use monopoly power to obtain free AI training data, several outcomes are possible. Google might be forced to either pay publishers for AI training rights, exclude more publisher content from its AI systems, or separate AI training from search indexing. Each scenario changes the publisher's leverage. A hypothesis: if Google must license content for AI purposes, the cost of serving AI Overviews rises, which could make Google less aggressive about showing them in place of traditional search results. That would restore traffic to publishers but reduce AI's visibility in Google's own interface.

This is not a near-term outcome. Mehta's questions do not constitute a ruling, and Google will likely appeal any unfavorable decision. But the judge's framing—that Google cannot simply call AI training a "product improvement" and exempt itself from antitrust scrutiny—suggests the legal ground is shifting.

## The asymmetry between search and AI is becoming untenable

[As reported in the same hearing](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/judge-in-penske-vs-google-says-ai-situation-seems-really-unfair/587216/), Jason Kint, CEO of the Digital Content Next trade association, attended the hearing and shared what happened. Google's attorneys described the traditional publisher-to-Google arrangement as a "vague historical course of dealing." Mehta rejected that characterization, indicating that if Google wants to use publisher content for a new purpose (AI training), a "vague" historical agreement is not sufficient legal cover.
