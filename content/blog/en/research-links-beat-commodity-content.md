Replacing commodity blog content with a research hub earned one B2B client over 1,000 citations in publications including The Wall Street Journal, Fortune, Axios, and Reuters, according to [Kevin Rowe, CEO of PureLinq, in a session at Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/how-we-earned-1000-links-geo-seo-recap/587850/). The turnaround took time—the first month produced exactly one link—but once journalists stopped waiting for pitches and started asking when the next dataset would drop, placements climbed month over month. The framework Rowe built works across finance, higher education, ecommerce, and B2B sectors because it replaces link building as a standalone tactic with a broader strategy: one piece of original research that earns links, brand mentions, AI citations, and social distribution at once.

## Original research works because journalists need it

The framework rests on a simple insight: [according to Search Engine Journal's report on the session](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/how-we-earned-1000-links-geo-seo-recap/587850/), commodity content can be published word-for-word by competitors, so it offers no reason for a journalist to call. Original research does. Rowe's argument is that five angles make a dataset worth a journalist's time. The most valuable part of his presentation was a single slide naming those five, and the student loan study that ran on two of them—pulling free federal data on student debt and landing full feature stories in Fox News and Fox Business—proves the principle works on budget.

The source data itself is rarely expensive. "It's really low hanging fruit and I think almost anybody can do it," Rowe said in the session. The real risk is what follows: free data plus a language model can make it easy to produce a study without rigor. Rowe was blunt about the cost: "AI slop is the biggest problem in research right now. If you send out slop to journalists and they catch you, and your data is wrong, they will probably stop working with you." Credibility matters more than volume.

## The research hub model: one link becomes 1,000+

The first case study covered a B2B brand with national reach and no physical locations, losing visibility to local competitors. Its blog was built on how-to commodity content—how to collect debt, top three financing tips. The team deleted it and rebuilt it as a research hub focused on original data.

Month one produced one link. Rowe said the client nearly quit. The breakthrough came when placements began climbing and journalists stopped sending inbound pitches to Rowe's team and started asking when the next dataset would be published. Somewhere in that stretch, The Wall Street Journal asked the team to update a dataset on its own. Over time, this single client accumulated more than 1,000 citations.

A full research hub is not the only path. A second case study showed an online university in a regulated category keeping its existing blog, adding a handful of studies alongside it, and putting its own professors in front of the media. That approach earned links from one-to-one pitching and a run of podcast bookings. Our recommendation: start with one dataset your team can update on a repeating schedule before committing to a full rebuild.

## Research ranks for commercial keywords and gets cited in AI Overviews

A fair objection to this strategy in 2026 is whether ranking still matters when AI Overviews answer the query on the results page. Rowe's answer was unambiguous: "We are not in the zero click world. 100% not. You can still get a ton of traffic from search organically."

His data backed it. Individual studies pull traffic from AI Overviews by themselves, and several hold position one for commercial keywords, not just informational ones. One client's office real estate study—which ranked U.S. financial districts by foreclosure risk—earned coverage in Axios Seattle, Barron's, and Wall Street Journal while ranking first for its target terms.

A hypothesis worth testing: original research cited as the primary data point in AI Overviews may drive more qualified traffic than unattributed answers. Rowe's plant science study, written by a Utah State University professor, ranks first for its target terms and appears to be cited as the primary source in AI Overviews. Our recommendation is to structure your research findings so that [AI answer engines can extract and cite them properly](/blog/structure-content-for-ai-answers). This step alone can shift you from mentioned everywhere to quoted nowhere—a gap worth [measuring in your own AI citation footprint](/blog/find-your-ai-citation-gap).

## The practical workflow: credibility, local angles, and methodology

Three tactical levers accelerated media pickup and rankings. First, putting a credentialed expert on the byline worked fastest. A study written by an academic or sector authority moved the needle more than generic bylines. Second, local news angles were powerful. Rowe's team surveyed homeowners with irrigation systems to learn what blocks outdoor water efficiency, then surfaced a concern those residents shared—giving local media a story with real stakes at city or state level. Third, real math in the methodology mattered. The real estate study scored and weighted every district by a transparent formula, making it defensible to journalists and useful to editors.

Rowe also addressed a common misconception about syndication networks. Syndication might boost exposure, but it does not generate link juice. "I wouldn't rely on that for the links. You want pickups," he said, meaning the value shows up only when a journalist picks the story up and writes about it independently. Without distribution muscle behind a study, topic and keyword selection have to be far more deliberate, since the research itself has to carry more of the burden.

## What to do now

- Pick one dataset your team can update on a repeating schedule (quarterly, monthly, or annually depending on your sector).
- Audit your last quarter of blog posts and flag every piece a competitor could have published word for word. Replace those with angles grounded in original data.
- Assign a credentialed expert as the primary byline—an employee with real credentials, a professor, or an academic partner.
- Pull your research methodology into a transparent, defensible formula that a journalist or AI system can verify.
- Map local news angles first; state and city-level data moves faster than national studies in early pitching phases.
