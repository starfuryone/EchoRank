## Visibility gets a brand into the conversation, but evidence decides whether it stays

Bill Hunt's article on [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/visibility-gets-you-on-the-list-evidence-helps-you-stay-there/592127/) makes a narrow but useful argument. Mention rates and recommendation shares are real metrics, but they describe only the first stage of a buying decision. In the case he reports, the brand was almost always in the consideration set, yet its pipeline was shrinking. The gap, in his reading, was evidence: AI systems knew the company belonged on the list but were less consistent at substantiating why it should stay there once buyers applied specific requirements.

This article separates what the reporting says, what we think it means for AI visibility work, and what remains unknown.

## What the case study reported

According to Hunt, the team built 38 criteria-based prompts across several product categories and buyer segments. These covered workflow fit, integrations, regulatory requirements, interoperability and analytics, and were run across five AI surfaces, producing 190 responses. He describes them as diagnostic prompts, deliberately longer and more specific than typical "best vendor" queries.

The headline numbers were strong. The company was mentioned in 98.4% of responses and appeared as a primary recommendation or shortlisted option in 93.2%. But when the responses were scored against the decision criteria in the prompts, 73.7% contained weak or missing evidence for important claims, and 15.8% of runs included a Significant or Critical Gap. Hunt notes that these gap numbers rose as his evidence-scoring criteria became more refined, which is worth keeping in mind when reading any single figure.

He also reports that weak evidence did not always mean missing capability. In several cases the product could do the thing, but the available material did not explain or prove it. A marketing bullet might assert an end-to-end process without showing the steps. An efficiency claim might have no test data or customer proof behind it. Sometimes the answer was spread across a product page, an integration page and an interoperability page, and the model had to join them itself.

## Why the two numbers don't contradict each other

A 98.4% mention rate and a 73.7% weak-evidence rate look inconsistent until you separate the stages. A mention answers "is this a legitimate vendor in this market?" Evidence answers "can this vendor satisfy this specific combination of requirements?" A model can know a company well enough to list it and still lack the detail to defend it against a checklist.

That is the distinction Hunt draws, and we think it is the most practical idea in the piece. It shifts the question from "are we visible?" to "for which buyer questions do we have enough documented proof to be kept in the answer?"

## What this means for AI visibility work

**Our recommendation:** treat evidence coverage as its own metric, separate from mentions and share of voice. Build a set of criteria-based prompts that mirror how procurement or technical buyers actually narrow a field, then score each response on whether the claims are supported, not only whether your brand appears.

**Our recommendation:** audit the path between pages. If an answer depends on connecting a product page, an integration page and a support document, check that those pages reference each other and state the specific conditions, limits and results. We have written about structuring content for this in [how to structure content so AI answers can actually use it](/blog/structure-content-for-ai-answers).

**Our recommendation:** collect evidence from outside the marketing site. Hunt's earlier argument, which he restates here, is that proof often sits with product, support, legal and customer success teams. Test results, implementation constraints and customer outcomes are the raw material for substantiated claims.

A short checklist to start:

- Write 20 to 40 prompts that include real constraints: integrations, regulations, volumes, operating conditions.
- Record whether your brand is mentioned, and separately whether each claim in the answer is supported.
- For each unsupported claim, find the page or internal source that should back it, and note whether it exists at all.
- Check whether the evidence is stated plainly on a page, or only implied across several.
- Re-run the same prompts after changes and compare the evidence scores, not just the mention rates.

## What is still unknown

The reporting is a single case study from one company, with one set of prompts. We cannot yet verify that the same 73.7% figure would appear in other categories, and the rise in gap rates as the scoring criteria were refined suggests the result depends heavily on how "evidence" is defined. Hunt's classification scheme is not published in the piece in enough detail for others to reproduce it.

We also do not know how individual AI surfaces weight or retrieve supporting material internally, and nothing in the source makes claims about that. The link between evidence coverage and actual pipeline is an inference from one company's experience, stated as a hypothesis rather than a measured effect. It is a reasonable hypothesis to test, not a settled result.

## Where to start

The practical takeaway does not depend on the exact numbers. Mentions tell you that you made the list. Evidence tells you whether you stay on it when a buyer starts asking detailed questions. Build a small criteria-based prompt set, score the answers for support as well as presence, and fix the gaps that buyers are most likely to ask about.
