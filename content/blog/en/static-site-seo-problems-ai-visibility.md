Static site frameworks like Astro, Hugo, and Gatsby are fast to build and technically clean. But they hand SEO work—and AI visibility work—entirely to you. A traditional CMS handles canonicals, sitemaps, redirects, metadata, and 404 pages in the background. With static sites, [every one of those becomes a job you own](https://ahrefs.com/blog/seo-for-static-sites/). That's not a problem if you know what to look for. But it's a serious problem if you don't, especially when AI crawlers depend on the same technical signals that search engines do.

## Duplicate URLs break both indexing and AI citation

Static frameworks and hosting platforms often disagree on trailing slashes. Your framework generates URLs with `/page/` while your host serves `/page`, or vice versa. The result is that the same content lives at two addresses. Canonical tags, internal links, and redirects then contradict each other about which version is canonical. Search engines split ranking signals between the duplicate. AI crawlers face the same problem—they can't tell which URL is authoritative, so they may pull citations from both or neither.

Our recommendation: Choose one format (with or without trailing slash) and enforce it everywhere. Configure both your framework and hosting to use the same convention. Point all canonical tags and internal links to the preferred version. Then crawl your site with a tool like [our site audit](/free-audit) to surface canonical conflicts, redirect chains, and internal links pointing to non-canonical URLs.

## Sitemaps include pages you marked noindex

On a static site, your XML sitemap is usually generated by code. The problem: the sitemap generator often doesn't know which pages you've marked as noindex. Drafts, thank-you pages, confirmation pages, and other non-indexable URLs end up in the sitemap anyway. No CMS plugin keeps everything in sync. A page marked `noindex: true` in your front matter can still appear in the sitemap unless you've explicitly built logic to exclude it. The same applies to RSS feeds.

This matters for AI visibility because crawlers check sitemaps as a roadmap to what you want indexed. A bloated sitemap with noindex pages creates noise and wastes crawl budget on pages that shouldn't rank or be cited.

Our recommendation: Build logic that excludes noindex pages from your sitemap generator. When you add a new page type or change how pages are generated, recheck that logic. Use your site audit tool to compare the URLs in your sitemap against indexability signals and catch pages that shouldn't be there.

## robots.txt doesn't get created automatically

Depending on your framework and deployment, a robots.txt file may not be created at all. You need to check that the file exists and contains the rules you actually want. Remember: robots.txt controls crawling, not indexing, so it's not a substitute for noindex. But it is critical for telling crawlers—including AI crawlers—which sections of your site to visit and which sitemap to use.

Static sites can also inherit vague or overly restrictive rules from default templates, accidentally blocking sections you want crawled. Our recommendation: Make sure robots.txt exists, points to the correct sitemap, and doesn't block any sections you want crawlers to access. Read our guide on [AI crawler user-agents and robots.txt](/blog/ai-crawler-user-agents-and-robots-txt) to understand which crawlers you should allow and why.

## Missing or unoptimized images harm crawlability

Some static frameworks include tools for resizing, compressing, and serving images efficiently. Many don't. Jekyll, for example, copies images as-is without automatic compression or resizing. Even frameworks like Next.js that provide an `<Image>` component only optimize images when you use that component. A raw `<img>` tag bypasses optimization entirely. Large, uncompressed images slow page load and consume crawl budget, which affects both search ranking and AI crawler coverage.

Our recommendation: Audit your image pipeline. Use your framework's built-in image optimization component if available. If not, add a plugin or image CDN. Check that every image is being processed, not copied raw. Ensure images are served in modern formats and resized for different devices.

## Schema markup gaps prevent AI extraction and citation

Static sites rarely auto-generate schema markup. Without structured data, AI crawlers can't reliably extract facts, context, author attribution, or publication dates from your pages. This hurts your chances of being cited. If a crawler can't extract a structured fact from your page, it's less likely to use it as a source.

Our recommendation: Add schema markup manually to your templates. Start with the types most relevant to your content: Article, NewsArticle, FAQPage, Product, Organization, or LocalBusiness. Use a schema validator to check that your markup is correct. Read our guide on [schema markup for AI answers](/blog/schema-markup-for-ai-answers) to understand which signals still matter when AI models are reading your content.

## Metadata consistency breaks across pages

A static framework doesn't enforce metadata fields across templates. One template might include `og:image` while another doesn't. Some pages get author metadata, others don't. Title tags and meta descriptions can be inconsistent. This fragmentation makes crawlers less confident in your content authority and reduces the metadata available for citations.

Our recommendation: Define a metadata schema in your framework. Create a single template or component for head elements that all pages inherit. Audit your output to verify every page has title, description, author (if applicable), publish date, and Open Graph tags. Use a crawl tool to spot inconsistencies.

## No automatic 404 handling or redirect management

A traditional CMS catches broken URLs and missing pages. Static sites don't. If a URL structure changes, you need to write redirects manually. If a page is deleted, you need to set up a 404 handler. Forgotten redirects create broken internal links and orphaned URLs that AI crawlers can't follow. Crawlers waste budget on 404 errors that a CMS would have prevented.

Our recommendation: Set up a redirect management system in your framework or hosting. Document all URL changes and the redirects that address them. Create a custom 404 page that helps crawlers and users find related content. Crawl your site regularly to find redirect chains (redirects that point to other redirects) and broken links.

## Deployment and cache layers add hidden indexing problems

Static sites are deployed as flat files, often through a CDN or static host. This can introduce cache headers, conditional serving, or cloaking that search engines and AI crawlers interpret differently. A page might be cached differently for browser versus crawler. Headers might inadvertently block indexing. The deployment process itself can introduce inconsistencies in how pages are served.

Our recommendation: Check the HTTP headers your hosting returns for every page type. Verify that cache headers don't prevent crawlers from seeing fresh content. Test your site with different user-agents to catch cloaking or conditional serving. Use a site audit tool to compare what crawlers see versus what browsers see.

## What you can do today

- Run a full site audit to find duplicate URLs, broken sitemaps, missing metadata, and canonical conflicts
- Audit your sitemaps against your noindex rules; remove any page marked noindex from the XML sitemap
- Check that robots.txt exists and contains the crawl rules you actually want
- Review your image pipeline and ensure all images are optimized
- Audit your metadata templates for consistency across page types
- Add schema markup to high-value content (articles, products, FAQs)
- Document your URL structure and set up a redirect system for changes
- Test your site with a crawler to see what AI models and search engines will actually find

The shift from CMS to static isn't inherently bad for SEO or AI visibility. It just means you need to be intentional about every part of your technical setup.
