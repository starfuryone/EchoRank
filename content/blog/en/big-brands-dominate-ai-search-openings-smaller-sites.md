## Big brands lead AI search, but only in certain query types

According to [research from BrightEdge reported by Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/big-brands-dominate-ai-search-but-leave-openings-for-smaller-sites/595397/), established travel brands occupy the vast majority of visibility in AI search results—but this dominance is not universal. American Airlines leads airlines at 26.6% visibility on Google AI Overviews, Hilton leads hotels at 28.3%, Royal Caribbean dominates cruise lines at 24.8%, and Expedia leads booking platforms at 31.3%. The gap is substantial: Expedia's visibility is 2.2 times higher than Kayak on Google AI Overviews. However, the data reveals a critical asymmetry. Only 9% of tracked AI search prompts have booking intent. That means 91% of queries fall into categories where smaller sites have genuine opportunities to appear.

## Inspiration queries favor human experience over brand authority

Smaller sites and user-generated content platforms perform significantly better when users ask subjective, experience-driven questions. [BrightEdge's findings show](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/big-brands-dominate-ai-search-but-leave-openings-for-smaller-sites/595397/) that YouTube, Reddit, and AAA are more prominent in inspiration questions, while booking platforms dominate comparison and booking-intent queries. This pattern suggests AI models recognize that personal experience, subjective opinion, and human judgment are more valuable for inspiration-type searches than brand authority alone. Travel blogs, niche review sites, and community forums can exploit this by creating content that centers on authentic experience rather than promotional messaging.

Our recommendation: audit your content mix. If your site focuses on "best budget hotels" or "how to book cheaply," you're competing directly against Expedia's 44% visibility in budget-related answers. If your site focuses on "what it's like to travel in [location]" or "hidden gems in [destination]," you're entering a category where big brands have deprioritized investment and AI models favor subjective content.

## Budget and deal queries are where brand centralization matters most

Booking platforms appear in 78% of Google AI Overviews answers for budget-related searches—queries containing words like "cheap," "deal," "budget," or "last-minute." Outside those search contexts, booking platforms appear in only 17% of answers. This concentration is not random. [As reported by Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/big-brands-dominate-ai-search-but-leave-openings-for-smaller-sites/595397/), booking platforms have optimized for commercial intent, and AI models reflect that optimization in their answer generation.

However, this is stated as a hypothesis: the pattern shows an observed association between these keywords and booking platform appearances, but does not prove that using these words causes a brand to appear. The mechanism by which AI models weight commercial intent is not publicly documented.

This creates a strategic implication. If your site cannot outbid established platforms for commercial searches, focus on non-commercial framing. Instead of "cheap flights to Barcelona," create content answering "how to travel to Barcelona on a tight budget" or "what it costs to visit Barcelona in low season"—angles that center on context and experience rather than transaction.

## Where smaller travel sites can compete: build your checklist

- Target inspiration and comparison queries rather than booking-intent searches
- Create content centered on human experience, subjective judgment, and authentic reviews
- Avoid head-to-head competition with booking platforms on budget and deal queries
- Focus on niche destinations, seasonal travel, or specialized travel styles (solo travel, family travel, adventure travel)
- Build depth in comparison-driven content, where [BrightEdge's data shows](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/big-brands-dominate-ai-search-but-leave-openings-for-smaller-sites/595397/) big brands feature more heavily but are not as concentrated as in booking queries
- Develop topical authority in a specific geography or travel segment rather than chasing broad keywords
- Use structured data and clear content organization so AI models can extract subjective insights from your writing

## The wider context: classic search is changing too

The dynamics of AI search visibility don't exist in isolation. [Google's Q3 earnings call revealed](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/googles-q3-earnings-call-casts-shadow-over-classic-search/595082/) that while query growth is at its highest in five years, referral traffic from Google to the open web has reached its lowest levels. Google is shifting Search toward task-driven, agentic experiences—helping users complete actions like booking reservations, filling forms, or scheduling appointments—rather than directing them to external sites for answers.

This transformation accelerates the trends BrightEdge observed. Transactional queries (booking, purchasing, reservation) are increasingly likely to be completed within Google's ecosystem. Informational and inspirational queries remain the primary pathways to external sites, which is precisely where smaller travel sites have an edge in AI search visibility.

We cannot yet verify how this agentic shift will change AI Overviews or ChatGPT's citation patterns, but the direction is clear: sites that provide answers to "what should I do?" questions will remain valuable to AI models. Sites that provide answers to "how do I book?" questions face structural headwinds from Google's business model.

## Why this matters for visibility and strategy

[The relationship between AI search visibility and traditional SEO is asymmetrical.](/blog/why-ai-recommends-your-competitors) Classic search rankings are heavily influenced by user behavior signals, and those signals feed upward into how AI models generate recommendations. But AI search does not produce ranking positions. [BrightEdge measures "share of voice"—the percentage of appearances across tracked AI answers—rather than individual rankings.](/blog/track-brand-mentions-across-llms) This distinction matters because it changes how you compete.

In classic search, you can rank #1 for a query and capture most of the traffic. In AI search, Expedia appearing in 44% of budget answers means Kayak, booking.com, Hotels.com, and others appear in the remaining 56%. The competition is fragmented, but the market share is concentrating around a few players.

For smaller sites, this creates an opportunity asymmetry. [You should focus on how to structure your content so AI answers can actually use it](/blog/structure-content-for-ai-answers)—clear topic sentences, logical organization, and direct answers. But you should also accept that big brands will dominate queries with commercial intent. Your competitive advantage lies in queries where big brands haven't optimized, or where AI models recognize that subjective expertise matters more than brand authority.

Travel bloggers, niche review sites, and destination authorities have a measurable path into AI search visibility. It requires accepting that you won't compete on "cheapest flights" but can own "best time to visit" and "local experiences money can't rank."
