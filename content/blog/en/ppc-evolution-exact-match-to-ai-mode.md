## Paid search has moved from granular control to algorithm-driven intent evaluation

Over the past two decades, PPC has transformed from a discipline that rewarded meticulous manual management into one shaped by automation and AI. [Greg Finn, co-founder of Cypress North, reflected on this shift](https://searchengineland.com/greg-finn-on-20-years-of-ppc-from-exact-match-to-ai-mode-486188) in a conversation with Search Engine Land, drawing on his experience starting in digital marketing around 2004. The early years demanded precise control: exact match meant exact, and skilled practitioners could construct tightly targeted experiences around individual searches. Today, that model has inverted. Broad match, once considered reckless, now evaluates context and intent using signals invisible to individual advertisers. Smart Bidding has replaced manual bid management. Google Ads automation has narrowed the gap between sophisticated and unsophisticated advertisers—and pushed marketers into auctions they may never have deliberately chosen.

## 2014 marked the start of advertisers losing granular control

The inflection point came in 2014 when Google required campaigns to use close variants for exact match. Until that moment, skilled advertisers could exercise precise control over matching, negatives, ads and landing pages. Once "exact" stopped necessarily meaning exact, Finn recognized what Google Ads was becoming: "That was the beginning of automation." Exact match's redefinition represented something larger than a technical change—it signaled that advertisers were beginning to surrender the control that had defined paid search for its first decade. The shift wasn't immediate or obvious. But looking back, it marked the opening of a door that has only widened.

## Modern broad match now evaluates context beyond literal keywords

Broad match has undergone the most dramatic transformation. Finn once helped write a book warning advertisers against using it. Today, he uses broad match across his accounts. The reason is not that he has abandoned caution—it is that broad match itself has fundamentally changed. Modern broad match can consider signals beyond the literal keyword, including previous searches and landing-page context. Combined with Smart Bidding, this means the system is no longer matching on words alone. It is matching on intent. "You're not doing keywords, you're doing intent," Finn said. Advertisers should evaluate outcomes rather than obsess over whether every individual search term appears perfect. Google may be using context that is not visible from the search alone.

## The expertise gap has narrowed as automation leveled the playing field

In paid search's early years, there was a much wider gap between advertisers who knew what they were doing and those who didn't. Tightly controlling keywords, ads and landing pages—and obsessively testing every variable—meant that skill translated directly to competitive advantage. "If you were good, it was easier," Finn reflected. Automation has changed that equation. Google can now make less sophisticated advertisers more competitive through Performance Max and AI Max, narrowing the space where expertise alone determines success. This has both costs and benefits. A small costume shop in Rochester, New York, could compete far beyond its local market using paid search when Finn helped manage its campaigns—a glimpse of how democratized the channel became. But that same democratization means the old paths to outsized advantage no longer exist in the same form.

## Smart Bidding emerged as PPC's most valuable innovation

When asked to identify the best PPC innovation of the past 20 years, Finn pointed to Smart Bidding. Its value lies partly in removing the tedium of manual bid management. Instead of repeatedly adjusting individual keywords, advertisers communicate the business result they want—a target CPA or target ROAS—and allow the system to determine auction-level bids. This doesn't mean blindly trusting Google. Advertisers and their competitors are, in effect, telling the same platform what they are willing to pay. But when underlying measurement is sound, automated bidding represents a major improvement over manually managing thousands of bids. The shift reflects a broader truth: marketers can object to automation, but eventually they have to learn how to succeed within it.

## What advertisers still need to manage manually

Despite the advance of automation, obvious negatives still require active management. Advertisers must monitor for irrelevant queries and adjust exclusions. But Finn's advice is nuanced: if a query looks unusual while the campaign is generating profitable results, marketers should consider that Google may be using context they cannot see. The balance has shifted from granular control over every element to outcome-focused monitoring. This requires a different skill set. It demands trust in systems while maintaining vigilance about results. It means measuring what matters—revenue, conversions, profitability—rather than whether every keyword match meets human expectations.

## What has been lost: Google's relationship with advertisers

One thing Finn misses from the early search era is Google's relationship with advertisers. In those years, Google was the challenger. Teams were smaller, representatives more consistent, and advertisers could interact much more directly with the people building the platform. As Google matured into a massive public company, that relationship became more corporate. The shift is understandable—a company at that scale cannot operate as a startup—but something was lost in translation. The direct feedback loop that once shaped product decisions has been replaced by algorithms and broad platform policies.

## Implications for AI visibility and citation

For marketers concerned with AI visibility and citations, Finn's evolution toward intent-based matching carries a lesson. AI answer engines increasingly retrieve and cite content based on context and relevance rather than exact keyword matches. Just as [you should structure content for AI answers to be used effectively](/blog/structure-content-for-ai-answers), you should also recognize that [AI often recommends your competitors instead of you](/blog/why-ai-recommends-your-competitors) because intent signals matter more than literal matching. The same shift happening in Google Ads—from keyword control to intent evaluation—is occurring in AI citation. Advertisers and content marketers who obsess over exact keyword placement while ignoring broader context and measurement are applying yesterday's logic to today's systems.

## Looking ahead: Traditional search may not survive the next five years

Finn believes the traditional Google search experience as we know it will not survive the next five years. That statement carries implications far beyond paid search. If the organic search interface itself is being reshaped by AI and personalization, the assumptions underlying both SEO and PPC strategies may need revision. The granular, keyword-focused approach that once dominated the channel is already obsolete. The question is not whether to adapt to automation and AI—that decision has already been made by the platforms. The question is how quickly you can shift from controlling inputs to measuring outcomes, from matching keywords to understanding intent, and from assuming you can see everything Google can see to trusting measurement systems that operate with more information than you have access to.
