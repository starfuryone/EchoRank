AI makes content production faster and cheaper. [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/how-we-build-an-seo-content-roadmap-for-the-ai-search-era-step-by-step/589944/) argues that speed and cost come with a risk: sacrificing quality and business value. The author, Corey Morris, walks through a six-step roadmap his agency uses to map content to measurable outcomes, research AI Overviews on every term, and decide which keywords are actually worth targeting. His core claim is that visibility in a SERP—or in an AI Overview—is worthless unless it converts. This article translates that roadmap for site owners and marketers preparing for AI search visibility.

## Step 1: Define the business outcome, not the visibility goal

Every keyword and topic must map to a measurable result. [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/how-we-build-an-seo-content-roadmap-for-the-ai-search-era-step-by-step/589944/) emphasizes that visibility itself is rarely the goal, except for media sites and ad-driven businesses. For lead-generation, ecommerce, or relationship-driven businesses, the outcome is conversion, revenue, or booked sales.

Morris uses a third-party logistics example: a 3PL provider is not seeking the lowest-cost shipper. They are seeking businesses with complex needs who will sign a contract and stay. For that business, visibility in a search result or AI Overview only matters if it generates a qualified lead that the sales team can close. Without that clarity, content investment becomes busy work.

Our recommendation: Before pulling a keyword list or running a competitor analysis, write down how you will measure success for each piece of content. What does a conversion look like? What is its value?

## Step 2: Build your keyword universe and competitor list

Construct a foundation by identifying your search competitors and the keywords they rank for. [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/how-we-build-an-seo-content-roadmap-for-the-ai-search-era-step-by-step/589944/) notes that search competitors often differ from traditional competitors. Your sales team may talk about different companies than the ones you compete against for search visibility.

The output here will include branded terms, off-intent results, and noise. You will need to filter manually. Morris walks through the 3PL example: the head term "3pl" has high search volume but is too broad. "3pl fulfillment" and "3pl companies" are lower volume but signal B2B intent—and they map to actual buyer conversations.

- Identify your true search competitors, not your sales team's competitors.
- Run a competitor keyword analysis to seed your universe.
- Filter for intent and relevance to your business outcome.
- Prioritize mid-difficulty terms with defined buyer intent over high-volume head terms.

## Step 3: Research AI Overview presence for each term

This is the step that separates AI-era roadmaps from traditional SEO planning. [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/how-we-build-an-seo-content-roadmap-for-the-ai-search-era-step-by-step/589944/) reports that research shows the top-ranking organic page loses roughly 58 percent of its clicks when an AI Overview is present. The same position on the SERP delivers far less traffic than it used to.

Morris found that an AI Overview appeared on nearly every B2B term his team evaluated for the 3PL client. This is not an automatic reason to skip a term. Instead, it is a decision point: Do you want to target that term knowing the click distribution has shifted? Will you write for the AI Overview itself, or optimize for the organic result below it?

For informational, top-of-funnel terms dominated by an AI Overview, you will get fewer clicks. Depending on your business model, you might prioritize brand awareness and citation over direct traffic. Or you might focus on the organic result instead.

Our recommendation: Treat AI Overview presence as a ranking factor in your priority matrix. Check each term manually or with a tool that audits SERP features. Decide per term whether the overview changes your targeting strategy or only how you write.

## Step 4: Use first-party customer data to anticipate AI citations

The exact phrases your customers use are the ones AI Overviews and LLMs pull from. [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/where-to-find-the-exact-phrases-that-get-your-content-cited-in-ai-search/591562/) emphasizes that your first-party data—conversations, ad copy, form submissions—already contains the language that AI models cite.

This is a hypothesis worth testing: if you monitor the language your prospects use across ads, chat, and your website, you can write content that anticipates which passages will be pulled into an AI answer. You cannot control LLM behavior, but you can make your content the most obvious choice for citation.

Our recommendation: Pull verbatim phrases from customer interactions, support tickets, and ad performance data. Use those phrases as anchors in your content outline. [We recommend checking your citation gap](/blog/find-your-ai-citation-gap) to see where you're mentioned but not quoted.

## Step 5: Make the go-or-no-go decision per term

After mapping to business outcomes, researching AI Overviews, and assessing your competitive position, decide which terms warrant investment. [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/how-we-build-an-seo-content-roadmap-for-the-ai-search-era-step-by-step/589944/) does not recommend skipping all high-difficulty or AI-dominated terms. Instead, weigh each term against your goals.

A hypothesis: terms with lower keyword difficulty and defined buyer intent will deliver better ROI than head terms, even if they carry lower absolute search volume. This is especially true in B2B, where the cost of a qualified lead often matters more than raw traffic.

## Step 6: Write with AI citation in mind

Once you have committed to a term, structure your content so that AI models can extract the right passage. This means [organizing information into answerable blocks](/blog/structure-content-for-ai-answers) and using language that mirrors customer intent.

## What remains unknown: standing instructions and ongoing monitoring

[Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/when-a-search-query-becomes-a-standing-instruction/591690/) reports that Google and OpenAI have both launched systems that keep search requests active after a user leaves. Google's information monitoring sends updates when data changes. OpenAI's dots are "always-on agents" that manage recurring tasks across connected apps.

We cannot yet verify how these tools will change citation patterns or how often your content will be re-checked by these systems. It is stated as a hypothesis that standing instructions and proactive agent monitoring could increase the value of evergreen content that handles comparison queries, tracking tasks, or change alerts. But the mechanics of how AI agents decide which sources to consult for updates are not yet public.
