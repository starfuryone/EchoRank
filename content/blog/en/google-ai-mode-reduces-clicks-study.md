A controlled study published in August 2026 proves that [Google's AI Mode reduces clicks to external websites](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/research-shows-google-ai-mode-sends-less-clicks-is-a-poor-user-experience/590221/) and delivers a worse user experience than standard search. Researchers from the University of Pennsylvania and Northeastern University tracked 1,100 users across a seven-day experiment and found that assignment to AI Mode significantly increased switching to competitor engines like Bing, DuckDuckGo, and Yahoo. The findings directly contradict public statements from Google's leadership that users love AI Mode and that it sends more traffic than ever.

## The Research Design and Key Findings

The study enrolled 1,444 participants and tracked 1,100 users' actual search behavior through a browser extension that recorded search patterns, clicks, and AI Overview visibility. Of those tracked, 956 completed post-experiment surveys. Researchers randomized participants into three conditions to isolate the effect of AI Mode on user behavior. The controlled setup allowed them to measure exactly what changed when AI Mode was present, absent, or hidden.

The core finding is stark: exposure to AI Mode significantly increased the likelihood that users would search on competitor engines. Users assigned to AI Mode also conducted fewer search sessions overall. When AI Overviews were removed or hidden, clicks to external websites increased materially. This demonstrates that AI-generated summaries on Google function as a substitute for visiting publisher websites, not a gateway to them.

Heavy users of Google Search experienced the strongest reduction in search activity when assigned to AI Mode. Lighter users saw smaller effects. The study found no comparable impact when users were exposed to search without AI features—removing AI Mode produced no detectable change in search session frequency or user perception.

## What This Means for Your AI Visibility

Our recommendation is to treat this research as a signal that AI Mode engagement differs fundamentally from traditional link traffic. If your site depends on referral clicks from Google Search, AI Mode's growth poses a direct threat to that channel. The study shows that users are *less* likely to visit external sites when AI Overviews answer their question on Google's results page.

This aligns with broader patterns we've documented in the AI visibility space. When AI systems answer questions directly, the incentive for users to click through to source pages diminishes. ## Google's Public Claims vs. the Research

Google's leadership—including CEO Sundar Pichai and Search head Liz Reid—has consistently stated that users are delighted with AI Mode, that they engage with it strongly, and that it drives more users to the open web than ever. [The research directly contradicts these assertions](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/research-shows-google-ai-mode-sends-less-clicks-is-a-poor-user-experience/590221/). Users assigned to AI Mode reported decreased trust in Google Search, lower perceived usefulness, reduced satisfaction, and less personal relevance compared to standard search.

Removing AI features did not make users perceive search as worse. This suggests that AI Mode's poor reception is not a side effect of novelty or adjustment but a response to the actual user experience.

The researchers noted obstacles they encountered during the study: while 94.7 percent of searches in the AI Mode group were successfully routed to AI Mode, Google's changes to AI Overview HTML during the experiment period broke the researchers' ability to reliably hide AI Overviews in the control condition. By the end of the experiment, their extension could hide AI Overviews only 51.1 percent of the time. This methodological challenge makes the measured effects even more noteworthy—the study detected significant differences despite incomplete isolation of conditions.

## The Disconnect Between Publisher Experiences and Google's Position

The findings reinforce what publishers across the industry have reported anecdotally: Google's AI Mode reduces referral traffic. This has been a consistent refrain from media outlets, review sites, and knowledge bases since AI Overviews rolled out broadly. The peer-reviewed evidence now quantifies what publishers have been observing in their own analytics.

A hypothesis worth considering is that Google's public statements about AI Mode reflect optimism about the feature's long-term potential rather than current performance. We cannot yet verify whether Google's leadership believes its own claims or whether the gap reflects different metrics—perhaps Google counts engagement on its own properties as "open web" traffic, or defines success differently than publishers measure it.

What remains unknown is whether Google plans to adjust AI Mode's design based on user feedback, or whether it will continue deploying the feature in its current form. The study suggests that AI Mode's structure—positioning summaries prominently and reducing incentive to click—aligns with Google's business model, even if it worsens the user experience.
